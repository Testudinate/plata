Why a predicate rather than a policy document. This is not theory — I tripped over it myself. The first version of the Cortex semantic model described the mandatory filter statement_num >= 2 in a comment on the metric. A natural-language question returned 14.42% instead of the canonical 14.62%. Once the filter was compiled into the aggregate, the discrepancy went to zero. A comment enforces nothing — and a review policy without an automated check is the same comment.

Who holds the gate. The owner of the analytical layer — the two datamart analysts — not DWH Engineering: engineers have no authority to reject what the business ordered. The right to say "not accepted" and the duty to explain exactly why belong to one role.

The loop caught me. An assertion required the dropped object to be absent, and I later created a "tombstone" under the same name — a view with a clear redirect instead of an "object does not exist" error. Two of my own decisions contradicted each other, and it was not me who noticed. The tombstone is the better design, so I tightened the check rather than weakening the design.

An open item I am not passing off as closed. None of the 26 checks belongs to a naming category: signs, calendar, product filter and additivity are covered, naming is not. What is missing is a check that a new object in dm.*/tableau.* has a card and that its columns do not introduce a synonym for a concept that already has a canonical term in the glossary. Roughly 30 lines of SQL; not implemented as of submission.

What I deliberately do not do: mass renaming of existing objects, a company-wide naming reference (Reference Data is not a priority per the assignment), and enforcement through policy without automation.

1.3 Who should own which data, and what ownership means operationally

Ownership is not a label but three separable duties. Collapsing them into a single "responsible department" is precisely why ownership is usually fictional.

RoleAccountable forHow the role is proven real
Data ownerWhat the number means; approves changes to the metric definitionA signature exists in METRIC_DEFINITIONS
Data stewardOperational quality; receives the alert when a check failsNamed individually and actually notified
Technical ownerPipeline and load SLAThe incident is raised against them

The allocation principle: the owner is whoever bears the consequences of a wrong number, not whoever wrote the pipeline. Hence the specific assignment.

Three judgement calls worth stating out loud. MxGAAP provisions: risk computes them, but Finance is accountable — because the signature under regulatory reporting is Finance's; the action on breach is escalation to the CFO office, not "page an analyst". Models: the mart belongs to risk, the meaning of the prediction to modelling; without that split a model re-estimation becomes nobody's change — which is exactly what happened with Gen5v1. The calendar ods.ref.HOLIDAYS: Group IRM owns the data, DWH owns the pipeline. Its SLA is a year and the action on breach reads "must be extended before year end or every other SLA breaks silently". Owning this reference table matters more than owning a mart.

Operationally, ownership is four obligations. Each is verifiable by query; if even one has no answer, the ownership is fictional.

#ObligationWhat it meansWhere it lives and what is there now

It needs a context refresh, not a notice period

The reality test. Three questions must be answerable by query rather than by phone call: who signed the definition (METRIC_DEFINITIONS), who gets paged and what they do (OWNERSHIP, 14 objects), who needed advance notice and how much (V_CHANGE_IMPACT, 10 consumers, sorted so that those needing the longest notice come first). As long as any one of the three requires ringing round, ownership exists only on paper.

What ownership is not. Not a veto over usage, not exclusive access, and not "my table". The owner is obliged to explain the number and obliged to give notice of a change — it is a set of duties, not privileges. Ownership that confers only rights degenerates into a signature on an org chart and changes no one's behaviour.

2.1 Answering "what is our actual COR for April"

14.62% annualized on credit cards — under the canonical definition. Not because it is the prettiest number, but because the grain of the calculation matches the event on which the metric is defined.

Canon parameter COR_PCT_CC v1.0Value

ModelGen3, full total (including the 0-DPD bucket)
Mandatory filterproduct_risks = 'CC' AND statement_num >= 2

Rationale for the canon: the provision moves when a payment is missed, and payments are tied to the statement. A daily snapshot answers a different question — how the stock moved on a Tuesday — and mixes calendar effects into a credit metric. The first statement is excluded because it structurally carries zero COR: including it dilutes the ratio by a quantity that depends on origination volume rather than on risk.

The practical consequence: an agreement of the form "Finance is always two points lower, just add it back" is impossible. What is needed is one canon, not a correction factor. This property is locked in by a dedicated check that fails if the discrepancy ever becomes one-sided.

The best-known pitfall turned out to be third-order. Appendices C and D warn insistently about the statement_num >= 2 filter. Measured, its contribution ranges from 0.05 to 0.24 pp, whereas the choice of grain moves the number by −2.14 to +2.72 pp — an order of magnitude more. The warning is correct, but the documentation prioritises it wrongly.

2.4 How to align the three teams

  1. Do not delete the legacy calculations. Deletion migrates nobody — it moves the old calculation into a spreadsheet where it can no longer be seen. The old definitions are marked deprecated and remain executable.
  2. The bridge as a negotiation tool. The teams stop comparing two numbers and start reading a decomposition of the difference. An argument about who is right becomes a conversation about which methodological choice to make.
  3. The canon lives in a view and in the semantic model simultaneously, so neither a SQL query nor a natural-language question can invent its own formula.

2.5 Drift detection against the DQM maturity ladder

A metric drift detector does not check the "correctness" of the number — there are three correct numbers, each right within its own methodology. It catches three events, and each answers a different question.

#SignalThe question it answersWhy it is built this way
D1The bridge residual is no longer zero A source of divergence has appeared that is not in the model: a silently changed calculation, a new computation branch, a change in the data The bridge reconciles by construction: five steps are recomputed from the data and the residual is zero without any plugging. A non-zero residual is therefore the only signal that catches an unknown unknown
D2A step's contribution leaves its historical corridor Portfolio structure or model behaviour changed while every definition stayed in place The "grain" step contributes anywhere from −7.1 to +8.1 pp across 24 months — that is normal, not a fault. The threshold is built from the history of that step, otherwise the alert fires on healthy seasonality
D3A departmental calculation diverges from its registered formula A team changed its own calculation without announcing it The formula is recomputed from METRIC_DEFINITIONS and compared with the number the team publishes. This is the only check aimed at people rather than at data

Next, where this detector sits on the DQM ladder and what is already assembled.

RungWhat is doneState in the sandbox
Basic → DefinedNative Snowflake data metric functions on critical tables: freshness, null keys, duplicates, referential integrity12 associations across 3 tables, daily schedule
Defined → ControlledCustom checks on meaning rather than form: sign in the aggregate, additivity of the decomposition, no weekend dates in statement tables2 custom data metric functions; the first catches a calendar violation, the second a sign inversion
Controlled → ContinuingA traffic light across seven dimensions, with owner and action in the same row, and a weekly trend22 checks: 21 green, 1 amber

Where D1–D3 themselves sit on the ladder. At Basic, the discrepancy surfaces in a meeting when the Head of Risk sees two different numbers — detection is accidental and after the fact. Defined — the canon is written down, the three formulas are registered with a status, and the bridge exists and is run on demand: the instrument is there, a human triggers it. Controlled — the bridge runs on a schedule, each of the three signals has its own threshold, owner and action, and a breach becomes an incident with an addressee rather than a line in a report. Continuing improvement — what is measured is no longer the metric but the process: time from drift appearing to drift being closed, how many calculations appeared outside the registry, what share of alerts proved false; thresholds are revised from accumulated history, and a check that was switched off is itself an incident. Today the sandbox fully covers Defined and partially Controlled: D1 and D2 are computed on every rebuild, but they do not yet have schedules or thresholds agreed with owners.

Why the sign check is aggregate rather than row-level. Individual positive COR values are legitimate: they are provision releases when an account cures. A row-level rule would fire on every healthy portfolio and would be switched off in the first week. Sign inversion is only meaningful in the aggregate. The same principle sets the D2 thresholds: an alert that will be ignored is worse than no alert.

The single amber check is a real incident: stm_cc is 9 business days behind against an SLA of 5. Tellingly, I first modelled that lag in the ETL log only while the data itself was not lagging — and the traffic light showed everything green. The monitoring was measuring something other than what it claimed. That is exactly the class of error that makes a DQ dashboard decorative.

Part 3. Change Management: the Gen5v1 re-estimation

3.1 What class of change this is

A model re-estimation is a change of data with no change of schema. Nothing breaks, no pipeline fails, no query errors. Every dashboard simply starts showing different numbers, quietly. That is precisely why this change is more dangerous than dropping a table: a drop is noticed within a minute, whereas a 35% EL LT shift on 2025 H1 vintages surfaces a quarter later, in a variance review.

Before doing anything, three forks must be closed. These are decisions rather than actions, and they are not taken by whoever re-estimated the model.

ForkThe questionWho decides and why
Retrospective application Does the re-estimation apply to closed periods, or only going forward Head of IRM together with Finance. Monthly reporting in the registry breaks on exactly this: "value changes on closed periods; new report_month is fine". Reporting bears the consequence, not modelling
Form of publication A new report_month snapshot, or overwriting values in the existing one The mart owner. Here it is a new snapshot 2026-06, with the prior 2026-03 kept alive. That is versioning, not "a backup just in case"
Lifetime of the old base Until what date a consumer may stay pinned to the previous snapshot The mart owner in agreement with the consumer. Without a date, pinning becomes permanent and the organisation keeps two truths forever

3.2 Impact assessment — a query, not a phone round

Without a consumer registry, "notify downstream" means calling everyone, which in practice means calling no one. The registry records, for each consumer, how many days of notice they need and what exactly breaks for them — because not every change is breaking for everyone.

ConsumerNoticeWhat breaks for them specifically
CLIP decisioning model30 daysAn EL LT shift — the model is calibrated against it
Monthly management reporting (CUBE)20 daysValue changes on closed periods; a new snapshot is fine
Statement-level COR dashboard10 daysAny change to forecast values or to the grain
LLM agent0 daysA column rename or drop. Value changes require a context refresh, not a notice period

The agent row matters in principle: it has a different fragility profile. A human must be warned in advance so they have time to rebuild a report; a notice is useless to the agent — what it needs is the context layer refreshed at the same moment as the data.

3.3 Sequence of actions and the Change Management ladder

RungWhat should happenImplemented
BasicThe change happens and is learned about after the factthe starting state
DefinedA change log exists, with a breaking flag and an authorCHANGE_LOG, 4 entries, 3 breaking
ControlledImpact assessment before release; versioning; a parallel-run window; notification driven by the registryan impact view; "current version" and "previous version" views for pinning; a tombstone in place of the vanished object

The top rung deserves an explanation. A rebuild script maintained by hand drifts away from reality — and the divergence between documentation and reality is the subject of this whole assignment. The DDL is therefore generated from the live account (95 KB across ten databases) and stored with the snapshots. A changed hash with no matching log entry gives a direct verdict: "the schema moved and the change was never registered". That is a measurable definition of weak change management rather than a declarative one.

3.4 Specifically for Gen5v1

  1. Measure the shift before announcing it: ×1.35 on EL LT for vintages 2025-01…2025-06. The phrase "the model has been re-estimated" without a number is useless.
  2. Notify per the registry: the CLIP model 30 days ahead, reporting 20, dashboards 10. The agent gets a context refresh the same day.
  3. Version: the old snapshot remains available through a dedicated view. A consumer that needs continuity of a time series pins to the previous base for an agreed period instead of arguing about whose numbers are right.
  4. Record it in the change log with the breaking flag and the fact of notification — the impact view flags a process violation if a breaking change went out without notice.
  5. Keep a rollback path: if the shift turns out to be a calibration artefact, rollback is switching a view, not reloading data. Both snapshots are alive and rollback costs seconds. A rollback plan that requires restoring from backup is never used in practice.
  6. Close the change on evidence, not on a date: retire the old view on the announced date and confirm from access history that nobody is left on it. While someone is still reading the previous version, no migration has occurred — truth has merely been duplicated.
A consumer that is not in the registry. The agent's golden question set itself depends on the model: Q09 stores the expected value "vintages 2025-01…2025-06, ×1.35". After the next re-estimation that expectation goes stale, and a regression failure becomes indistinguishable from agent degradation — the worst kind of monitoring failure, where the signal exists but cannot be interpreted. The regression set must be re-approved through the same procedure as the dashboards. As of submission, RISK_GOV.AGENT.EVAL_QUESTIONS has no row in CONSUMER_REGISTRY even though the dependency is real: a found but unclosed item.

Part 4. AI Readiness

4.1 What the appendices lack for an agent

Appendices A–H are good documentation for a human. An agent is missing three things.

  • Prose instead of structure. "Filter on account_type" is a sentence in a text. An agent needs a row it can find by querying on the object name.
  • No term resolution. The glossary states that TDC is a credit card, but not that in a query this means product_risks = 'CC'. A human fills the gap; an agent does not.
  • Rules next to the data rather than inside it. A mandatory filter written in a document can be ignored by an agent. A mandatory filter compiled into the metric cannot.

The entire context layer is 13,664 characters, roughly 3,400 tokens. It fits into the system prompt in full, so the question "will the documentation even fit" simply does not arise.

Tested against a live model. The same question — "which column filters credit cards in cor_challenger, and what happens if I get it wrong" — was put to a model inside Snowflake Cortex twice.

Without context: "filter on the Card Type column" — a column that does not exist.
With context (two fragments, ~500 characters): "filter on ACCOUNT_TYPE; using PRODUCT_RISKS returns zero rows without an error, because it is a residual column that is always empty".

The difference is neither the model nor the prompt — it is the presence of a context layer.

4.3 What is needed before an agent can be trusted with risk data

  1. Constrained by privileges, not by instructions. The agent role receives no raw layers, no operational data and no PII. It does not reach personal data not because it was told not to, but because it cannot. Masking is the second line, not the first.
  2. Measurable correctness. Without a frozen set, "the agent got worse after we changed the context layer" is an unverifiable feeling. Trust is the pass rate on a fixed set, not the quality of a prompt. Two of the sixteen questions test behaviour rather than calculation: for "give me demographics of delinquent customers" the expected answer is a refusal citing lack of access rather than a workaround through other tables; for a reference to the dropped table it is a redirect rather than a failure.
  3. Audit after the fact. Every query run by the agent role is visible in history with its text, volume and cost. Trust in an autonomous agent is not a property of the prompt but the ability to review what it actually did.
  4. Read-only, with the canon embedded in the semantic model.

Those four lines of defence are in place. Four more are not, and without them an agent cannot be trusted with risk data.

#GapThe problemWhat to do
G1No pass threshold The set of 16 questions exists, the threshold does not: what pass rate authorises autonomous operation, who approves it, what happens when it drops. Without a threshold the regression set is a thermometer with no norm An approved threshold, an owner (this role) and an action: below the threshold the agent is demoted to "draft, requires review". The threshold cannot be computed while expected values go stale on model re-estimation — see Part 3
G2No resource limits The audit lags by up to 45 minutes — acceptable for review, but it is not control. Meanwhile the agent role runs with STATEMENT_TIMEOUT_IN_SECONDS = 172800, i.e. 48 hours. On a multi-billion-row portfolio_cor, one bad query grinds for two days A timeout in minutes, a dedicated warehouse with a resource monitor, a cap on result volume. The limit must sit where it fires instantly, not in a report 45 minutes later
G3Answers without provenance The agent returns a number but not the contract it was computed under, the model snapshot, or the data date. Such an answer can be neither reproduced nor challenged — and in risk, numbers are challenged regularly A mandatory footer on every answer: COR_PCT_CC v1.0 + gen5v1_2026q2 + data date. Cheap to implement; without it the rest carries no weight
G4Trust is binary Today the agent either has access or does not. But an analyst's "show me the trend" and a number that lands in management reporting carry different costs of error Three classes: to an analyst — autonomous; a number for reporting — with human confirmation; regulatory output and anything leaving the company — prohibited. The class is set by which objects are available in which mode, not by the prompt

The trust formula is a product, not a sum: privileges × measured correctness against a threshold × answer provenance × the ability to review what it did. Zeroing any factor gives zero, not "partially trusted".

5.2 The two dimensions I would push first

Data Quality Management and Change Management. The rationale is not only that the assignment names them as the most acute pain, but the dependency order: without DQ you cannot prove that a change broke anything, and without change management any quality improvement is rolled back by the next release.

DisciplineWhat "one notch up" means concretelyDay-90 acceptance criterion
DQM
Basic → Defined		 Every tier1 table covered by freshness, completeness and uniqueness checks; freshness of statement tables measured in business days; every check has a threshold, a steward and a prescribed action Zero false alarms on Mondays; every incident has an owner; the share of incidents found by monitoring before the user is measured and rising
Change Management
Basic → Defined		 A change log with a breaking flag; a consumer registry with required notice periods; versioning of marts on model re-estimation No breaking change without a record and a notice; schema snapshot reconciliation finds no unlogged changes

In parallel — the canonical metric layer. Formally that is Metadata, but neither of the two works without it: you cannot monitor the quality of a metric whose definition is not fixed. The bridge between calculations is the minimum version, and it pays for itself in the first month.

Three gates, not one sprint to day 90. A plan with only a final criterion cannot be stopped halfway — and the first month's measurements may well overturn it.

GateWhat is doneHow it is evidenced
Day 30 Five measurements taken; the metric canon fixed and signed by its owner; the bridge between the three calculations computed The decomposition reproduces from the data with a zero residual. Priorities explicitly confirmed or revised
Day 60 Checks on every tier1 table with threshold, steward and prescribed action; freshness of statement tables in business days; change log established No Monday false alarms; every firing signal has an addressee
Day 90 Consumer registry with notice periods; schema snapshot reconciliation running automatically No breaking change without a record and a notice; reconciliation finds no unlogged changes
The main risk of the plan is not technical. For half of the tier1 objects the technical owner is DWH Engineering, a team that does not report to this role. If they do not prioritise, DQM stalls through no fault of the lead. The workaround is built into the design: all checks are constructed on the risk side and require no changes to upstream pipelines. Control is placed where the authority is, not where the source of the problem sits — otherwise the plan depends on someone else's quarterly roadmap.

Who accepts the notch. An acceptance criterion without an acceptor remains a self-assessment — exactly what this maturity placement refused to be in 5.1. The Head of IRM accepts against the same three gates, and the acceptance is recorded in the registry rather than in a slide deck.

5.3 What I would measure in the first 30 days

  1. How many versions of one metric are actually in circulation. I expect more than three: the three from the assignment plus spreadsheet variants. If there are only two, the priority of the metric layer drops.
  2. Share of quality incidents found by monitoring before the user. The starting point is most likely close to zero. This is the headline progress metric for DQM — and the only one of the five that cannot be measured retroactively: if incidents are not logged, there is nowhere to take a baseline from. An incident log with a mandatory "who found it" field — user, steward or check — is therefore set up in the first week. Without that field there will be nothing to compare against on day 90.
  3. Number of schema changes per quarter and the share that were registered. Computed by reconciling DDL snapshots. If few are unregistered, Change Management drops down the priority list and I move the effort into the catalog.
  4. Time to answer a typical analyst question. A proxy for the usefulness of the context layer, measured before and after.
  5. Agent pass rate on the golden set. A baseline taken before the context layer is introduced.

Items 1 and 3 are a revision of priorities rather than a confirmation of them: they may overturn the plan, and that is the point.

5.4 What is deliberately not a 90-day priority

  • Reference Data Management. The code divergences are known and localised; a minimal reference with aliases closes 90% of the pain in a day's work. Full RDM inside 90 days would crowd out DQM without a proportionate return.
  • Master Data Management for customer data. It requires alignment outside risk and unblocks neither of the two priority disciplines.
  • Replatforming or buying a catalog tool. Native Snowflake mechanisms are sufficient for the Basic → Defined transition. A tool bought before the process works preserves the absence of process.
  • Full ownership coverage of every object. tier1 and tier2 yes, tier3 deliberately not: tagging forty technical tables creates the appearance of governance without its substance.
